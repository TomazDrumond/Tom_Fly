FlyRank ML Internship — Capstone Research Paper

Reading the Refresh Queue

Scoring content refresh opportunity from Search Console signals — and why a hand-written rule and a forward-looking model disagree about which pages matter most.

Author: Tomaz Drumond Lane: Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring Data window: March–April 2026
01

Abstract

Content teams cannot manually review every page in a large site, so this project asks which pages deserve refresh attention first — the exact priority action FlyRank's own published research names as its top recommendation, backed by a striking but exploratory finding (3.2x health boost from refreshing mature content). A transparent baseline rule — pages with meaningful search volume but underperforming click-through relative to their ranking position — was built and hand-audited against two real signals from FlyRank's production flags. A Logistic Regression model was then trained on the same March 2026 features to predict a genuinely forward-looking outcome: whether a page's click-through rate would decline into April. Evaluated on a client-holdout split, the model beat the baseline at both Precision@20 (0.55 vs. 0.50) and Precision@50 (0.70 vs. 0.54). The model's error pattern reveals a specific blind spot — it under-detects decline in already low-ranked pages that lose visibility entirely, rather than merely converting worse — which the ranked recommendations below are built to account for.

02

Introduction

The decision this work supports is narrow and concrete: which pages should a content reviewer look at first, this cycle, out of far more pages than they have time to check by hand. The action a reviewer takes on a flagged page is to refresh, expand, protect, prune, or monitor it. A wrong call has a real cost in both directions — a false positive spends limited review time on a page that wasn't actually a priority, while a false negative lets a real decliner keep losing visibility unreviewed, uncaught until the next review cycle.

With thousands of pages and one reviewer's limited capacity, a ranked queue with transparent reason codes gets the right pages in front of a human far faster and more consistently than manual triage — this is decision-support, not automation. Nothing in this paper claims to predict how Google's ranking algorithm works, or that refreshing a flagged page will causally improve it; the claim is narrower and more defensible: which pages are statistically more likely to need attention, based on patterns observed in past data.

The FlyRank case study

This project is a direct case study of FlyRank's own published research. FlyRank's State of AI-Driven SEO (March 2026) names "refresh mature pages before they decay" as its top priority action, citing a striking 3.2x health-score boost and 57x impression increase for refreshed 365+ day pages. But that same report is explicit about a limit in its own exploratory ML appendix: importance and accuracy figures there are described as "descriptive rather than causal," and the reported holdout split does not confirm whether it accounts for repeated brands across rows. This paper takes that exact operational priority — refresh scoring — and builds it into a fully validated, reproducible scoring system: a transparent baseline audited against real signals, a model compared against that baseline on the same client-grouped split, a dedicated validation audit stress-testing the split design itself, and a leakage check on the final feature set. The goal is not to critique FlyRank's published findings, but to demonstrate what the next layer of rigor looks like when applied to the same underlying business problem they identified.

03

Data

All data comes from the FlyRank/internship-warehouse release on Hugging Face — a monthly-partitioned warehouse of Search Console performance joined against content and client dimension tables. Three tables were used:

  • fact_content_daily_performance — daily page-level GSC metrics, partitioned by month. Two partitions were used: month=2026-03 (features) and month=2026-04 (label window).
  • dim_content — static page attributes (word count, content type).
  • dim_clients — client-level access and history metadata, used to build the availability filter.

What was excluded, and why

  • fact_content_query_90d — its fixed 90-day window (Apr–Jun 2026) overlaps the sealed test month, creating leakage risk for any feature developed during this iteration phase.
  • *_sample table — this is not a random sample; it is exactly the sealed final month (June 2026), reserved as a held-out test period and never used to develop label logic.
  • dim_content.content_updated_date — discovered during data-contract verification to be a current extract, not a point-in-time March snapshot: 84% of rows showed an "update" date after the March snapshot date, producing impossible negative day-counts. Staleness was dropped from every score in this paper as a result.
  • GA4 engagement fields — adoption varies per client (ga4_data_start differs across the panel), and naive inclusion would silently treat "no data yet" as "zero engagement." Deferred to future work.

Coverage limitation

Only 36.7% of the March daily-performance rows have gsc_data_available IS TRUE. Per-client coverage is highly uneven: of 55 clients, 8 have 0% GSC availability in March and none have 100% — the median client sits at only 8% availability. Every result in this paper implicitly reflects the higher-coverage subset of the panel, not the full client base, and that should be read as a real constraint on generalization, not a footnote.

04

Methodology

Signal verification

Before encoding any rule, two signals behind FlyRank's real production flags were checked against the data directly.

Signal 1 — Staleness vs. CTR

Mixed

No pages in this slice are older than 365 days, and CTR does not decline cleanly with age — it spikes at 90–180 days (0.0201) before dropping at 180–365 days (0.0022), contradicting the refresh flag's "older content underperforms" assumption. Given the small sample in those buckets (n=1,325 and n=261), staleness was excluded from the final rule entirely, reinforced by the content_updated_date data-quality issue found in Section 3.

Signal 2 — Position vs. CTR

Confirmed

CTR falls steadily and substantially as position worsens — 0.0106 at positions 1–3, down to 0.0019 at position 20+ — backed by large samples throughout (16k–82k rows per bucket). This is the reliable signal the baseline rule leans on.

Baseline rule

A page is worth reviewing if it has meaningful search volume (100+ impressions in the month) but a click-through rate below the mean CTR of other meaningful-volume pages at the same position tier — meaning the problem is the page itself, not its ranking. Reason code: underperforming_ctr. Action: review_for_refresh.

Label definition

The baseline's CTR-underperformance rule is a same-window comparison — useful as a rule, but not a fair supervised target, since it cannot say anything a reviewer couldn't already see by looking at March data directly. The model instead predicts a genuinely forward-looking outcome: whether a page's CTR is lower in April than it was in March, trained only on March features. This is a real prediction task rather than a restatement of the present.

Validation design

The panel (100,893 page-months across 43 clients, visible in both March and April with meaningful March volume) was split by client, not by row — 30 clients in train, 13 in test, with zero client overlap. A random row-level split would let the model learn client-specific quirks rather than a generalizable pattern; a prior held-out experiment on this same lane showed exactly that gap (in-sample Precision@20 of 0.550 dropped to 0.400 on a genuine client-holdout). Train and test declining rates were close (0.443 vs. 0.438), confirming the split is representative rather than accidentally skewed.

Method: Logistic Regression, evaluated via Precision@K — the metric fits a ranking problem ("which pages first"), not plain classification. Simplicity was chosen deliberately: a readable model that clearly beat the baseline made a stronger comparison than reaching for Random Forest or Gradient Boosting without first showing the simple version earns its complexity.

Validation audit

The grouped-split discipline above was independently re-tested by rebuilding the same model under a naive random split and comparing it against the honest grouped-split result. The gap confirmed the same pattern seen earlier in this project: Precision@20 dropped from 0.80 (random split) to 0.55 (grouped split) — a 25-point overstatement concentrated exactly where a reviewer looks first. A second audit deliberately added a label-derived feature (April's own CTR) to the model to check whether the resulting score would spike toward-perfect, the standard leakage tell. It moved only marginally (Precision@50: 0.70 → 0.72) — a result that, on inspection, reflects a feature-scaling artifact in this particular linear model rather than genuine safety, since the label is deterministically related to that feature by construction. The practical conclusion: a feature is excluded because it encodes future information by definition, not because one specific model failed to exploit it — the final feature set contains no April-window or future-derived inputs regardless of what any single audit run shows.

Error analysis

False negatives (3,920) outnumber false positives (2,069) roughly 2:1 — the model is more prone to missing real decliners than to over-flagging healthy pages. The clearest failure pattern: high-volume, top-position pages with strong March CTR were flagged as near-certain decliners (score ≈ 1.0) purely on the ceiling-effect logic, when April CTR actually held flat. Conversely, low-volume pages already ranked poorly (position 80–90+) were scored as low-risk, then collapsed to zero clicks in April — a real blind spot, since the model treats low CTR as a floor rather than accounting for a page losing visibility entirely.

06

Limitations & honest framing

  • This is observed, decision-support ranking, not a causal claim — nothing here proves that refreshing a flagged page will improve its performance, only that similar past pages showed decline.
  • The label (CTR decline, March→April) is a proxy for "needs a refresh," not the true outcome of refreshing a page.
  • Results reflect the higher-GSC-coverage subset of a 55-client panel, not the full client base (Section 3).
  • Evaluated on a single month-to-month transition (March→April); performance on other transitions or seasons is untested.
  • This does not reproduce, predict, or reverse-engineer Google's ranking algorithm.
07

Ranked recommendations

The model was scored across the full 100,893-page panel and translated into four actions with transparent reason codes. Of the panel, 2,609 pages (2.6%) were flagged review_for_refresh — a capacity-sized queue, not an unworkable flood — spanning 29 of 43 clients (67%). A consistency check confirmed zero pages carry both the highest-urgency action and the lowest-confidence reason code, meaning the action logic never contradicts its own stated confidence.
